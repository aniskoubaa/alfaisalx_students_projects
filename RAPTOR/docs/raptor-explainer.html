Jetson Orin NX · Onboard perception

Finding people from the air, in three tiers.

RAPTOR is a search-and-rescue quadcopter. Its onboard computer has to answer three questions about every person it sees — is someone there, what posture are they in, and what is happening to them. Those three questions cost wildly different amounts of compute, so they get three different answers. Everything runs on the aircraft, with no network.

The one decision everything hangs on

A 3B vision-language model on an Orin NX produces tens of tokens per second. A 60-token description costs seconds, not milliseconds. It cannot run at 30 fps — so we never ask it to. Tiers 1 and 2 run on every frame and decide when it is worth spending those seconds.

Why the expensive model is gated

The difference between running the VLM on every frame and running it on events is not a tuning detail. It is the difference between a system that works and one that falls over in the first second.

The gate is a bounded priority queue, four jobs deep. When it is full we drop the low-value refreshes and keep the new-person and posture-change events. The detector node and the VLM node are separate ROS 2 nodes on separate CUDA streams, so a slow inference can never stall perception — and if the VLM node dies outright, the system degrades to posture-only reporting, which is still operationally useful.

01 Detect

Is there a person?

YOLO11-pose, exported to TensorRT at INT8, with ByteTrack for identity. Every frame, around 30 fps, no exceptions.

One model, not two: the pose model already emits person boxes, so running a separate detector alongside it would double the cost for nothing. ByteTrack was chosen over re-ID-based trackers because it keeps low-confidence boxes alive — which is precisely the regime small aerial targets live in.

Licence check, before we build on it

Ultralytics YOLO is AGPL-3.0. Fine for an open academic project; contagious the moment RAPTOR ships to anyone without source. If that is a possibility, the permissive escape hatches are YOLOX, RT-DETR, or NVIDIA TAO — and the time to decide is now, not after a semester of work.

02 Posture

Standing, or lying down?

This is a geometry question, and we answer it with geometry — not by asking a language model.

Given 17 COCO keypoints, the angle of the torso vector (shoulder midpoint to hip midpoint) tells you the posture directly. That computation costs effectively nothing on top of the pose model, is stable frame to frame, is explainable to an operator, and cannot hallucinate. Asking a 3B VLM the same question costs seconds and occasionally invents a confident wrong answer.

But there is a catch, and it is the reason this tier needs a model of our own.

That is the argument for training this one small model ourselves. Normalise the keypoints (origin at the hip midpoint, scaled by torso length, rotated by measured roll), add per-keypoint visibility because occlusion is informative, and train a two-layer MLP on {standing, sitting, crouching, lying, unknown}. It is under 100 KB, runs in microseconds on the CPU, needs only a few thousand labelled frames — and it is the genuinely novel, publishable piece of the project.

Why we have to fine-tune on aerial data

A person lying down, seen from 50 metres up, is about ten pixels of dust-coloured texture. COCO has no idea what that is.

This is also why fine-tuning is not optional. Stock YOLO trained on ground-level photographs will systematically miss prone people from above — the exact failure we can least afford. The fix is a few thousand labelled frames from datasets built for this view (HERIDAL, SARD, Okutama-Action, VisDrone) plus our own flight footage over our own terrain, which is also what we calibrate the INT8 engine on.

03 Describe

It has to be a VLM, not an LLM

A text-only language model cannot see the camera. The best it could do is paraphrase YOLO's labels — turning person, 0.87, lying into “a person is lying down”, which adds nothing and can only introduce error.

Everything that makes this project worth doing — dark staining on the right trouser leg, an arm bent at an unnatural angle, trapped under a collapsed awning, waving at the camera — lives in pixels that no detector describes. That needs a model that takes the image itself as input.

Ground it, then constrain it

Two rules keep a small generative model honest. First, never send the raw frame and hope — send the tracked person's crop with a 30% context margin, plus what tiers 1–2 already established: posture, immobility duration, altitude, view angle. Second, force structured output against a fixed schema with grammar-constrained decoding, so we get parseable fields instead of prose:

FieldExample value
appearanceadult, light shirt, dark trousers
body_positionprone, left arm extended above head
surroundingsgravel, near a low wall, no other people
injury_indicators["dark staining on right lower leg"]
signallingnone observed
not_visible["face", "right arm"]

That last field is deliberate. It gives the model a legitimate place to put its uncertainty instead of inventing detail to fill a sentence.

How it is wired

Five ROS 2 nodes. The rates on the edges are the point — one lane runs flat out, one fires a few times a minute, and nothing in the fast lane ever waits on the slow one.

Recording every topic to rosbag2 on every flight is what makes the rest of the project tractable. Benchmarks replay identical bags instead of demanding new flights, and the same bags become the labelled dataset — complete with the synchronised GPS and attitude that the gravity-aligned classifier depends on, which cannot be recovered after the fact.

Build it, fine-tune it, or just use it?

You asked whether designing our own models would be better. Partly — and being specific about which parts is what separates a good project from a wasted semester.

Pose estimator NoProbably notYes COCO keypoints generalise well enough, and a pose model is expensive to retrain for marginal gain.

The honest framing

Transfer learning from a strong pretrained backbone is not a shortcut — it is what a professional team would also do. Spend the saved effort on the three things that are actually ours: aerial domain adaptation, gravity-aware posture reasoning, and the triggered-VLM pipeline.

What RAPTOR is not

This system points a camera and a generative model at injured people. Being precise about its limits is part of the engineering, not paperwork around it.

  • ×Not a medical device, and not a triage system. A VLM reporting “dark staining on the leg” is an observation about pixels, not a finding of haemorrhage. It must never be described otherwise — not in the UI, not in a paper, not in a demo.
  • ×Not a decision maker. Every victim report is a recommendation to a human, who decides what happens next.
  • ×Not a surveillance tool. No face recognition, no re-identification across flights, no demographic labelling. Track IDs are per-flight and are not identities. That is a design boundary, not an oversight.
  • ×Not a guarantee that an area is clear. A blank map means the system found nothing, which is not the same as nothing being there. The altitude–recall curve exists so operators know the difference.
  • ×Not in the flight loop. The Jetson reads telemetry from MAVROS; it never writes control commands. Enforced in code, so a perception bug can never become a flight safety incident.

The triage flag is rule-based, deliberately

The VLM produces observations. The escalation decision is made by explicit rules over structured fields — for example lying and immobile for more than 60 s and a non-empty injury_indicators list raises urgent. Every flag carries a plain-words reason, so an operator can see why and disagree, behaviour is auditable after an incident, and a model update cannot silently change the escalation policy.

Those thresholds are placeholders. They need review by someone with actual search-and-rescue or emergency-medicine experience before any real use.

Every number on this page is a target

No figure here came out of a measurement yet — the frame rates, latencies and pixel heights are budgets and models, not results. The benchmark plan exists precisely to replace them, and until it does, nothing here belongs in a report as a finding.
